    How the matcher works

    This is the part that ports straight to Swift or Kotlin unchanged, and the part worth getting right.

    • Text is normalised — lowercased, punctuation stripped.
    • Every brand carries aliases: each way the name is actually printed on a tag.
    • A second pass collapses the characters OCR habitually confuses — 0/o, 1/l/i, 5/s, rn/m — so 1u1u1emon still matches.
    • Then edit distance on individual words, for the rest.
    • Strong hit answers outright. A weak one becomes Not sure with suggestions, never a confident wrong answer.

    Typing, with typos

    Search is a separate pass — a few characters someone is still typing, not a blob of OCR text. It tolerates the mistakes people actually make:

    • Swapped letters. raplh lauren, vuroi, shien — the most common typing error there is, and the reason this uses Damerau rather than plain edit distance.
    • Wrong or missing letters. adiddas, nrth face, carhart, colombia.
    • Initials. tnf finds The North Face.
    • The other country's spelling. under armor finds Under Armour.

    A clean prefix always outranks a fuzzy hit, so four letters into lulu you get lululemon — never a near-miss on something else. When a match came through an alias, the row says which one, so it isn't a mystery.

    Where the admin went

    There was an on-device admin panel here — a passcode, a hidden gesture, an Admin tab — and it has been taken out entirely. The brand list is now a constant in the source, and the app never writes to it.

    Because the honest version needs a server anyway. A passcode that ships inside the app is visible in view-source and readable in a binary’s strings; it hides a panel, it does not secure one. That was tolerable while the list lived on one phone and the worst case was somebody editing their own copy. It stops being tolerable the moment the same screens reach other people’s email addresses, payout handles and money owed.

    So his half is a separate sign-in against a real database, on its own address, with none of its code in the app customers download. Same data, two front doors. The schema and the endpoints for it are in BACKEND.md.

    What the server holds

    Profiles, shipments and the buy list all move to a database — that is what the Account tab is for, and it is the reason a label can exist at all: a carrier key cannot live in a browser, so something has to sit between the app and Shippo.

    The box itself deliberately stays on the phone. A half-packed box is a draft, not a record, and syncing one would mean writing to a server on every tap for something nobody else will ever read.

    Four tables and six endpoints, in supabase/schema.sql and netlify/functions. The app runs without any of it today.

    Accounts, and what they are for

    An account is required before a box can be sent, and it earns that: the label, the shipment and the payout all have to hang off one record. Without it every parcel is anonymous and the only way to answer “where is my money” is to search an inbox.

    No password anywhere. Sign-in is a link by email. A password is a thing that can leak, and somebody selling a coat twice a year will not remember one — so the app asks for an email, a name and where to send money, and nothing else. It never asks for a card number, and it should not start.

    Scanning still forgets. Nothing records what anybody looked up, whether they have an account or not.

    Logo matching, realistically

    Reading text and recognising a swoosh are different technologies. Text recognition is a free, instant, on-device API. Logo recognition is one of four things, and they are not equivalent:

    • Cloud vision API — ~$1.50 per 1,000 scans, needs a network, and its brand coverage skews to global giants. It knows Nike. It won't know Vuori or Alo, which are where the margin is.
    • Multimodal LLM — accurate and flexible, but a few cents a scan and a second or two of latency. Constrain it to pick from the brand list or say nothing, or it will confidently invent a brand.
    • A trained model — free per scan, offline, and needs 50–200 photos per brand. That's the wall most of these projects hit.
    • Image feature-print match — the one I'd build. iOS has VNGenerateImageFeaturePrintRequest: it fingerprints an image and measures distance between fingerprints, on-device and free. Needs three to five reference photos per brand, not hundreds. A new brand means uploading photos, not retraining.

    All four need reference photos he doesn't have, and with nothing leaving the phone the app can't gather them for him. So they get added by hand, three to five shots of each brand's real tags, from whoever maintains the list. Collecting those is the long pole on any of the four, which is worth knowing before picking one.

    What I'd check next

    Tags with no text. A woven logo and a size, nothing to read. That's image classification, a different and much harder problem — and it's why the Not Sure path needs to be good rather than an apology.

    Whether scanning beats typing. Three letters into the search box already answers the question. The camera has to be faster than that, or it's the demo and search is the product.

    Category comes from the brand record, not the photo — nobody should accidentally build garment recognition.
